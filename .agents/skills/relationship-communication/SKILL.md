# Relationship communication

Use this skill when the user writes about being misunderstood, misunderstanding a partner, blunt or indirect communication, conflicting interpretations, or a discussion escalating before both people feel heard. Pair it with [`relationship-analysis`](../relationship-analysis/SKILL.md) for the relationship cycle, [`emotional-analysis`](../emotional-analysis/SKILL.md) for the user's experience, [`nervous-system-overload-analysis`](../nervous-system-overload-analysis/SKILL.md) when capacity is reduced, and [`actionable-support`](../actionable-support/SKILL.md) when a ready-to-use script or next step would help. In daily bundles, contribute the communication analysis to `agentic/relationships.md`; put a concise copyable aid in `agentic/support.md` when warranted. For standalone notes, use the facade's sibling `<note-stem>-analysis.md` output.

## Relational aim and user-specific context

The user has stated that his aim is a lasting relationship where both people can communicate directly, state basic needs, clarify intentions and feelings, understand one another, and repair misunderstandings rather than fight unnecessarily. Treat his stated intention to understand and not harm as a good-faith starting hypothesis. Do not treat intent as proof of impact, make it an exemption from accountability, or assume that every disagreement is trivial.

Historical chat records his preference for direct, factual, structured language and his explicit rejection of overfamiliar or sentimental “babying.” In this request, he describes his own style as sometimes dry and blunt, and wonders whether this may relate to autism-spectrum-like social processing. Keep that as his hypothesis, not a diagnosis or established cause. Consider other explanations too: individual style, stress, timing, channel, fatigue, culture, relationship history, or the specific words used. Do not presume that his partner is neurotypical or that either person's communication style represents a whole group.

Keep the tone soberly hopeful, respectful, and practical: warm enough to signal collaboration, never patronizing or artificially intimate. Do not mistake “direct” for “cold,” but do not pretend direct wording has no interpersonal effect.

The user reports that feeling misunderstood during escalating conflict can coincide with stomach clenching, the “hot anvil,” defensiveness, somewhat raised voice, and more gesturing. Treat these as his reported early-warning cues, not proof of a particular autonomic state. Ask about the partner's account rather than inferring what she feels or intends.

## Workflow

1. **Clarify the event and the problem type.** Use the smallest relevant excerpt or the user's account. Separate an observable statement/action from each person's interpretation. Decide provisionally whether this is a misunderstanding, a real disagreement, a boundary/impact issue, or a mixture. A repair can clarify meaning without requiring agreement.
2. **Separate intent, wording, reception, and impact.** Record the user's stated intended meaning; the words and delivery actually reported; what the partner says she heard or experienced; and any impact. Label the partner's view `UNKNOWN` unless provided. Do not translate “I meant no harm” into “no harm occurred,” or “it hurt” into “harm was intended.”
3. **Treat style mismatch as bidirectional.** Direct or literal phrasing can be clear to one person and sound cold, critical, or final to another; indirect hints, implied meanings, or softened refusals can also be hard to interpret. Test these possibilities with each person's account. Do not frame one partner as deficient or make one person solely responsible for translating.
4. **Map the escalation loop without assigning a villain.** For example: ambiguous cue → interpretation → attempted clarification/defense → partner response → increased urgency or withdrawal → each person's interpretation hardens. Mark each step as report, direct observation, framework lens, inference, or unknown. Do not force the historical “guilt/collapse” loop onto a new incident.
5. **Check capacity before solving.** If the user reports the hot anvil, stomach clenching, rising voice, sharper or faster gestures, inability to listen, or an urge to force resolution, stop adding theory and reduce the exchange to one sentence. Offer a pause only as an option; agree on a specific return time if both want one. A pause is not abandonment, punishment, or a way to avoid returning. If anyone feels intimidated or unsafe, prioritize space and safety; do not recommend pressing the conversation, blocking movement, or touching the other person.
6. **Offer a concise two-way clarification.** Use the least loaded wording that preserves the user's actual need. A useful sequence is: (a) name one observable event; (b) state the user's interpretation as an interpretation; (c) state feeling and need; (d) give one request; (e) explicitly invite correction and the partner's account; (f) reflect back what was heard before rebutting or adding context. Validation means accurately recognizing the other's reported experience, not agreeing with every claim.
7. **Close with repair and a concrete next step.** If delivery had an impact, acknowledge the specific behavior (for example, raising volume or continuing after capacity was lost), its possible impact, and what will change. Keep the original issue visible as well: do not let either person's distress erase the other's concern. Distinguish clarification, apology, boundary negotiation, and agreement; none automatically substitutes for the others.

## Copyable communication scaffold

Adapt this to the actual event; do not fill blanks with guessed feelings or send it automatically:

> “I want to understand this, not win the exchange. When **[observable event/words]** happened, I understood it as **[my interpretation]** and felt **[my feeling/body cue]**. What I meant to say was **[intention]**; I can see that the wording or delivery may have landed differently. The need I am trying to communicate is **[need]**, and my specific request is **[request]**. What did you hear me saying, and what was happening for you? I’ll reflect your answer before I explain more.”

If capacity is too low:

> “I want to resolve this, but my body is overloaded and my voice is rising. I’m pausing now so I don’t make this harder. Can we return to this at **[specific time]**? The issue still matters to me.”

Do not use either script to compel disclosure, agreement, forgiveness, reassurance, or immediate response. Adjust it to the partner's preferred channel and pace when known.

## Evidence and limits

Back every substantive psychological or neurotype-related conclusion with relevant professional literature and cite it near the claim. State whether the support is an empirical finding, a clinical-model lens, or an inference. Name relevant limits in population and task; if no source directly supports a couple-specific conclusion, label it as a tentative formulation or practical experiment, not established fact. Literature can inform the method but cannot decide what happened between these two people.

- Research on autistic/non-autistic interaction supports considering mismatch and mutual adaptation rather than assuming an individual deficit. In a story-transmission experiment, mixed-neurotype chains lost more detail than same-neurotype chains; separate brief-interaction studies found lower rapport ratings for mixed pairs. These are group-level findings from constrained tasks, not evidence that this user is autistic or an explanation of this couple's conflict: [Crompton et al. (2020), information transfer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7545656/); [Crompton et al. (2020), rapport](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.586171/full).
- An experimental study found higher negative affect and physiological arousal following invalidating than validating responses under stress. Use this to motivate accurate reflection, not to equate validation with agreement or to claim that one phrase will resolve a conflict: [Shenk & Fruzzetti (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2011.30.2.163).
- Couple-interaction research associates demand/withdraw patterns during problem discussions with greater relationship distress, while the pattern's direction varies by context. It supports mapping a cycle rather than assigning a fixed pursuer/withdrawer role; association does not establish cause in an individual couple: [Eldridge et al. (2007)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17605544/).

Use these studies as evidence-informed guardrails, not as a clinical assessment, a verdict on either partner, or proof that this skill is therapy. Recommend a qualified couples therapist when the conflict is recurring, impairing, hard to interrupt, or either partner wants guided work.
