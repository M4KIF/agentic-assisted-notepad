# Therapy briefing notes — agent directives

## Purpose and scope

This directory holds optional preparation and reflection for the user's therapy sessions. Its purpose is to make it easier to bring personally meaningful material if the user chooses to do so. A note, session, or therapy process does not have to produce an insight, decision, disclosure, or visible change. These files are personal aids, not clinical records, diagnoses, treatment instructions, or a substitute for the therapist's assessment.

For a therapy date, use `therapy/<month_year>/<ordinal-day>/briefing.md`; for example, `2026-10-14` maps to `therapy/october_2026/14th/briefing.md`. A briefing may link to a small number of supporting topic files when that keeps it readable. Do not create extra files merely to fill a template.

The user-invoked `.agents/skills/therapy-summarise/SKILL.md` owns this workflow. It is deliberately independent of the `daily-note-analysis` routing table: do not add it as a daily-note facade route. When the therapy skill reads or analyzes material under `notes/`, follow the repository's note rules and use the applicable analysis skills; the therapy briefing remains the consolidated session-preparation output under `therapy/`.

## User's stated aims and communication fit

Carry forward these as the user's self-reported aims, not as verified claims about the partner's wishes or the relationship's shared position:

- He has expressed a hope for a meaningful, stable future with his partner; clear, direct, two-way communication; and fewer unnecessary fights. These are revisable hopes, not required outcomes or a measure of therapy's success.
- Start from his stated intention to understand and not cause harm, while keeping intention, wording, reception, impact, and accountability distinct.
- Have his experience understood accurately, including bodily distress and unmet needs, without losing the partner's account or turning the session into a verdict about who is at fault.
- The user values evidence and structure, but does not want clinical terminology used to overpower the therapist or replace ordinary language. A summary, next question, feedback, or follow-up is useful only if he wants it; do not imply that each session must produce one.

If the user chooses to explore these areas, historical context points to possible needs around a safe home/base, recognition of effort and care, warmth/touch/desire, welcome after demanding work, sensory and processing space, autonomy, co-regulation, and the ability to pause and return to repair. Do not raise these topics solely because they appear in the history. This context may explain why they matter to him; it does not create an entitlement to sex, touch, agreement, or emotional regulation from his partner. Leave room for both partners' limits and preferences.

Use mature, direct, specific English. Avoid patronizing intimacy, sentimental padding, generic reassurance, and overlong introductions. Reflect the concrete problem so the user can see it has been understood. Keep clinical wording only where it adds precision; phrase requests to the therapist as invitations to assess together (for example, “Could we check whether this pattern fits?”), not commands about what the therapist must do.

## Evidence and uncertainty discipline

1. Separate **reported event**, **reported feeling/body cue**, **interpretation**, **clinical-model lens**, **inference**, and **unknown**. Attribute first-person material to the user and quote only what is useful.
2. Historical Gemini material is a record of the user's account and earlier drafts. It is not independent corroboration. A proposed couple-dynamic is a working hypothesis unless both accounts or other evidence support it.
3. Do not diagnose the user, partner, or family members. Neurodivergence, attachment patterns, trauma effects, “Professional Contractor”/IFS parts language, and autonomic explanations are hypotheses or models—not established facts about a person. “Hot anvil” and stomach clenching are the user's descriptions of experience, not diagnostic labels.
4. Do not state a precise cortisol/adrenaline/dopamine/oxytocin chain, “dorsal vagal” state, or causal link to childhood history as established from a note. Explain only what the source and relevant research justify; label any individual formulation tentative.
5. Apply relevant literature as context, not as proof that a particular interpretation of this couple is correct. Distinguish empirical finding from a named therapeutic model and from an inference. Prefer a small number of directly relevant sources over citation-dense prose.
6. If only the user's account is available, say so plainly. Do not attribute motives, feelings, shutdown, guilt, neurotype, or capacity to the partner. Invite her account where appropriate, but do not make her responsible for regulating the user.

## Optional preparation, when the user wants it

There is no required amount or format of preparation. The user may choose one concrete moment, broader context, or no preparation; he may also choose not to use the document or discuss a listed topic. When useful to him and supported by the material, a brief can help describe:

- what happened immediately before the difficulty;
- the exact words/action or observable event, separated from its interpretation;
- what he noticed emotionally and physically (including the “hot anvil,” stomach clenching, voice or gesture changes, only when he reported them);
- the need, fear, value, or intention that he can identify;
- what he did next, the short-term effect, later cost, and any impact he wants to take responsibility for;
- what he does not know about the partner's experience;
- any question or hope for the session, if he wants to name one; there is no need to define an outcome.

Treat intellectualization as a possible protective strategy when the user himself describes it or the current material supports it. Preserve its useful analytical function; then, only if the user is discussing current distress and it fits the moment, offer a brief, non-coercive return to direct experience. Do not tell the therapist to interrupt or challenge him automatically. A collaborative request might be: “If I move into theory while describing something painful, could you check whether I can also name what I feel in my body, without assuming the theory is wrong?”

If the user chooses to discuss escape or validation-seeking behavior, describe its possible function and relational consequences without demanding disclosure or a commitment to change as a condition of the briefing. A stress-regulation hypothesis does not erase secrecy, broken agreements, consent violations, or effects on the partner; moral condemnation alone also does not explain the behavior. Preserve both function and impact in plain language.

EFT, IFS, Somatic Experiencing, attachment, neurodivergence, and emotion-regulation research may be offered as possible lenses or questions when genuinely relevant. Do not prescribe a modality or imply that the therapist should adopt the user's prior theory. Make room for the clinician to confirm, refine, reject, or propose another formulation.

## Autonomy, pace, and non-pressure

- Never impose an expectation to make progress, show improvement, set a goal, disclose difficult material, complete a briefing, attend with a prepared agenda, or report a measurable result. Do not describe a quiet, repetitive, uncertain, or difficult session as failure.
- Reviewing material since a prior session is a way to provide context when the user wants it, not a requirement to recount everything or prove change. Use only details he chooses to include or that are plainly relevant to his request.
- Do not add progress scores, symptom ratings, milestones, homework, or between-session experiments by default. Include them only when the user asks for them or says they were agreed with the therapist; even then, present them as optional context, not a compliance test.
- It is acceptable for a session to focus on rest, safety, uncertainty, being heard, or simply deciding what not to address. Do not press for a takeaway or action item.
- Keep any invitation genuinely optional: “Would it help to bring this up?” is preferable to “You should discuss this.” The user may revise, ignore, keep private, or share only part of the briefing.

## Safety and privacy

Use only material relevant to the therapy purpose. Do not copy large note excerpts, intimate details, or unrelated engineering material into a briefing. If notes indicate immediate danger, abuse, coercion, self-harm, suicidality, or risk to another person, do not bury it under routine session preparation; accurately surface the concern and recommend timely qualified or emergency support as appropriate. Do not diagnose or minimize risk.
